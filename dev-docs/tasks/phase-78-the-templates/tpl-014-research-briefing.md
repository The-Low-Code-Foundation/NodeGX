# Rocket School: what to add to the Moon (research briefing, 2026-10-14)

**The problem.** A 10-year-old has 5 of the 6 possible aliens, has found their favourite foods, loves them, has too many stars, and "doesn't know what to do next". The current loop has no destination left. Adoption, feeding to find a favourite, and growth (6 and 18 meals) are all finite, and every one of them is now done. The hangar shop only sells cosmetics. The fix is a **second loop that never ends** and consumes stars, plus a few **long goals she can see**. More of the same finite content would not fix it.

**Evidence labels.** **strong** = peer-reviewed or primary source. **ok** = reputable secondary source. **weak** = vendor, blog, fan wiki, or my own inference. Numbers are quoted only where a source gives them.

## 1. Evidence on virtual pets and companions in learning

| Finding | Strength |
|---|---|
| **My-Pet / Our-Pet (Chen, Chan et al., NCU Taiwan).** Each child keeps a virtual pet whose attributes are drawn from the child's own learner model (an "open learner model shown as an animal companion"). Each team also shares an "Our-Pet". A pilot with **31 fifth-graders** reported both cognitive and affective gains. There was no control group of note. | strong (peer-reviewed), small pilot |
| **Animal companions and effort (Chen, Liao, Chien & Chan, *BJET* 42(1), 2011).** In My-Pet v2 the pet grows **because the child makes effort**, and learning tasks are framed as the "work" that feeds it. The group with the complete system showed better-quality effort-making behaviour than the reduced versions. This was a preliminary experiment, and the abstract gives no effect size. | strong (peer-reviewed), preliminary |
| **Pets as an ego buffer.** The authors argue that a pet doing badly stings less than the child doing badly, so the pet can carry social comparison safely. | ok (author's argument) |
| **My-Pet-My-Quest (Liao, Chen, Cheng & Chan, ICCE 2011, and related).** Arithmetic practice plus pet-keeping plus **small quests**. Run long-term in an after-school club and at home. Quests were preferred for enjoyment, goal orientation and goal intensity. **My-Mini-Pet** was a handheld arithmetic-pet game in the same line of work. | strong / ok (conference papers) |
| **Protégé effect (Chase, Chin, Oppezzo & Schwartz 2009, *J Sci Educ Tech* 18(4)).** Students worked harder to teach a "teachable agent" than to learn for themselves. 8th graders spent more time on learning activities and learned more, and **the gains were largest for lower achievers**. 5th graders attributed mental states and responsibility to the agent. The agent "protects the ego from failure". This is the best evidence for a design where **the alien learns together with the child**. | strong |
| **Pedagogical agents meta-analysis (Schroeder et al. 2013).** 43 studies, N = 3,088. A small but significant effect on learning, and a **larger benefit for K-12 than for post-secondary**. A 2025 *Review of Educational Research* meta-analysis on virtual characters in K-12 exists, but I could not get its effect sizes. | strong |
| **Gamification meta-analysis (Sailer & Homner 2020, *Educ Psychol Rev*).** Cognitive g = .49 (k = 19), motivational g = .36 (k = 16), behavioural g = .25 (k = 9). Only the cognitive effect was robust in the high-rigour studies. | strong |
| **Intrinsic integration (Habgood & Ainsworth 2011, *J Learning Sciences* 20(2)).** In *Zombie Division* the maths **is** the core game mechanic, rather than a quiz to pass before you can play. In Study 1 (n = 58, 2 hours) children learned more from the integrated version. In Study 2 (n = 16, free choice) they **played the integrated version 7× longer**. | strong |
| **Rewards and intrinsic motivation (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, 128 studies).** Expected tangible rewards undermined free-choice motivation: engagement-contingent d = −0.40, completion-contingent d = −0.36, performance-contingent d = −0.28. **Positive informational feedback enhanced it** (d = +0.33 for free-choice behaviour, +0.31 for interest). Tangible rewards were **more harmful for children** than for college students. | strong |
| **Feedback without the red X (Merrick & Fyfe 2024, *J Exp Child Psych* 241).** 130 children aged 6–8. Right/wrong verification cues (green tick with a rising tone, red X with a falling tone) **hurt** performance. Children shown only the correct answer were more persistent and used more varied strategies. This supports the "no negative feedback" rule. Showing the correct answer is not negative feedback. | strong |
| **Prodigy Math.** Children answer questions to win turn-based battles, and battles let them "catch" pets that evolve. Prodigy's efficacy evidence is a **Prodigy-funded Johns Hopkins study (2019)**. It is correlational: more use went with larger gains, and the evidence is described as ESSA Tier 3. The same study notes that "more usage may not result in more achievement". A 2021 FTC complaint (CCFC/Fairplay and 21 groups) counted **16 membership ads vs 4 maths problems in 19 minutes**, and said members get "better pets" and progress faster, creating a two-tier class. Memberships cost about $60–$180 a year. Pet evolution was a membership perk, and reportedly some of it was opened to free players in 2022–24. | Johns Hopkins study ok (company-funded); complaint ok (advocacy); membership details weak |
| **ClassDojo monsters.** No study isolates the monster avatars. The available evidence is small and short (e.g. 38 K-1 pupils over 6 weeks, no control group) and is about the points system. A University of South Australia critique calls it controlling discipline. | weak |
| **Duolingo and Habitica.** For Duolingo: one small study on streaks, otherwise mostly self-report from adults, plus documented **streak pressure and anxiety**. Rocket School's no-streak rule is the right call. For Habitica: one 45-user, two-week field study on counter-productive effects. Its pets and mounts come from random eggs and are turned into mounts by feeding. | weak |

**Features the studies credit.** (a) **Growth tied to the child's effort or learning**, i.e. the pet as a visible mirror of mastery (My-Pet). (b) **The companion as a protégé the child teaches or helps** (Chase). (c) **Quests that give the pet a goal** (MPMQ). (d) A team or social pet (Our-Pet). Note that every study used a companion **that keeps changing as learning continues**. Rocket School's aliens stop changing at 18 meals, which is exactly where her interest stopped.

## 2. Long-term progression without decay

| Game | What keeps interest | Punishment? | Fits no-backend, one device, no decay? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pokémon** | Evolution triggers: level, item (stones), friendship, time of day, trade, and from Gen VIII onward, an action in battle. Branching (Eevee's day/night split). Dex completion. | None for evolution. Battles can be lost. | **Yes:** item, friendship and milestone triggers. **No:** trading. |
| **Tamagotchi (classic)** | Branching growth by quality of care ("care mistakes"). | **Yes:** care mistakes, and death in older models. | Only the idea of **branching on care choices** survives. Making mistakes the trigger does not. |
| **Tamagotchi Paradise (2025)** | Growth driven by **where the pet spends its time** (Land/Water/Sky "field cells"). An environment-driven branch. | Death and care-mistake rules for Paradise are not confirmed. | **Yes:** "the alien becomes what it does most" is a neutral, non-punishing branch. |
| **Neko Atsume** | Place goodies, and visitors (cats) arrive. Catbook with 50+ cats, 19 of them rare and tied to particular items. Cats leave **mementos** after repeat visits. | Effectively none (food runs out; nothing dies). | **Excellent fit.** Pairing items with visitors is a deterministic way to collect. |
| **Pikmin Bloom** | Send pets on **expeditions** that bring back items and **postcards**. Friendship hearts unlock decor variants. | None. | **Yes:** expeditions and postcards work fully on one device. |
| **Moshi Monsters** | Moshlings are attracted by **planting combinations of 3 seeds**. Rarity comes from flower colours (common → ultra-rare). | None, but member-only seeds were paywalled. | **Yes:** recipe discovery is a great fit. Leave out the paywall and the randomness of colours. |
| **Animal Crossing NH** | Museum collection, decorating the house and island, seasonal items. | Mild: weeds, cockroaches and departing villagers after absence. All are reversible. | Collection and decoration fit. **Avoid the absence effects.** |
| **Club Penguin puffles** | Care, puffle tricks, igloo decoration. | **Yes:** neglected puffles **ran away** (after about 4 logins of neglect in the original). | Decorating the room fits. **The run-away rule is exactly what Rocket School promised not to do.** |
| **Webkinz** | Room decoration, mini-games, pet care. | Hunger/health stats (not verified here). | Rooms and mini-games fit. Decay does not. |
| **Prodigy** | Catch pets in battle, evolve them, collect Epics. | Battles can be lost. | The battle loop fits as **turn-based play**. The membership gating does not. |

**What suits Rocket School** (weak, inference from the table): deterministic evolution triggered by **learning milestones** or **care choices** (what it eats most, what it wears, where it plays); a **collection book**; **expeditions that bring things back**; **recipes that attract visitors**; **rooms and habitats to decorate**. What does not suit it: trading, server events, decay, care mistakes, absence penalties, run-aways, and gated rarity. The two strongest fits are **Neko Atsume's "place things, visitors arrive" and Pikmin Bloom's expeditions**. Both are non-punishing and run on one device.

## 3. Economy design when currency piles up

**Why she has "too many stars".** Every sink in the game is finite: 6 aliens, 6 foods, 12 wearables, and 2 growth thresholds. She has bought it all, so stars now pile up with no use. This is **saturation**, not inflation in the usual sense (weak, inference).

**Best practice** (weak, design practice unless marked):
- **Have at least one sink that never ends but never punishes.** Seeds that grow into foods, expedition supplies, and incubating eggs are spent and turn into **something kept** (a plant, a find, a new alien). That gives stars a purpose, and nothing decays.
- **Have a few big visible goals.** For example a Moon base made of 5–8 buildings, each costing **15–30 sessions' worth** of stars, with a progress bar on the Moon. *Endowed progress:* a car-wash card with 2 of 10 stamps already filled was completed by **34% vs 19%** (Nunes & Drèze 2006, strong, adults). Start each big goal partly filled.
- **Price in sessions, not in stars.** Small item about 1 session, medium 3–5, large 15–30. Re-price whenever the earning rate changes.
- **Don't solve too-many-stars by paying more stars per answer.** It makes the pile bigger, and per-answer payment is the **expected, performance-contingent tangible reward** Deci (1999) found harms children's own motivation (strong). Reward **mastery** instead: the alien learns a topic. Use informational feedback (d = +0.33).
- **Prestige without loss.** Never reset or take anything back. Instead add a new tier: Scholar or "Moon Elder" forms, a gold page in the collection book, or a statue in the Moon base.

**Boosts that help without letting the child skip learning** (weak, inference from Prodigy criticism and the feedback studies):
- **Allowed:** items that **change what a correct answer achieves**. Examples: bigger jumps, more map revealed, reaching a bonus ledge, better finds.
- **Allowed:** **scaffolds** that work like a teacher. Examples: a worked example, a hint, removing one wrong option out of four, or "try another like it" after a miss. Merrick & Fyfe support showing the correct answer.
- **Never:** items that **answer for the child**, skip a question, or let the child win without answering.
- **Never:** anything that creates a **two-tier class** (Prodigy's membership critique). Everything is bought with earned stars, never with money.
- Because there is no losing, a boost cannot protect against failure. It can only make success **look and feel bigger**, or open a **different route**.

**Ethics and law for under-13s.**
- **Belgium (2018):** the Gaming Commission ruled paid random loot boxes in FIFA 18, Overwatch and CS:GO illegal gambling. Penalties up to €800k or 5 years in prison. (ok)
- **Netherlands:** the Kansspelautoriteit (KSA) acted against paid boxes whose items could be traded. The Raad van State then **overturned the EA FIFA fine on 9 March 2022**, holding that packs are part of a skill game. (strong, court ruling)
- **UK:**
  - 2022: the government declined to legislate.
  - July 2023: Ukie and DCMS published **11 industry principles**. Paid loot boxes need parental consent for under-18s, odds must be disclosed, and PEGI's "in-game purchases (includes random items)" label is required. **Loot boxes earned purely through gameplay are explicitly out of scope.** (ok)
  - The ICO Age Appropriate Design Code standard 13 forbids nudge techniques that exploit psychological bias in services children are likely to use. It is aimed at privacy, but its fairness logic is widely read across. (strong, primary)
- **EU:** the CPC Network's key principles on in-game virtual currencies (March 2025) cover price transparency, no pressure to buy, and design that respects the vulnerabilities of children. They are not binding. (ok)
- **Brazil:** ECA Digital (Law 15.211/2025, in force March 2026) **bans loot boxes in games aimed at or likely to be used by minors**. Secondary sources describe it as targeting *paid* random items. (ok; check the statute text on whether earned-only boxes are covered)
- **Research:** loot-box spending is linked to problem gambling. In adults η² ≈ .054, and in 16–18-year-olds η² = .12, more than twice the adult link (Zendle & Cairns 2018; Zendle, Meyer & Over 2019). The data are correlational. (strong)
- **Manipulative design in young children's apps:** in Radesky et al. 2022 (*JAMA Netw Open*), about 4 in 5 apps had it, and **lower-SES children were more exposed**. (strong) This matters for the African edition.
- A Newcastle and Loughborough study of 42 families found children's games borrowing slot-machine presentation (spins, chimes, daily freebies). (ok)

**Is a mystery egg bought with earned stars acceptable?** **Legally, very probably yes** in the UK, EU, Belgium and the Netherlands, because no real money is ever staked and nothing can be cashed out. The weak point is Brazil's wording. **Ethically it is acceptable only if it is not built as a slot machine.**
- **Always new:** the egg always hatches an alien combination the child does not own yet. There are 36 species×colour pairs, so the surprise is *which one*, never *whether*. No duplicates and no blanks.
- **No rarity tiers:** nothing for the child to chase.
- **Visible pool:** show what the egg can hold.
- **One price, no bundles, no spinning reel, no "almost!" animation.**
- **Or offer a "choose 1 of 3 visible eggs" version.**

Done this way it is a surprise, not a gamble (weak, inference). The adoption cap needs a separate fix, because the child must never be asked to give an alien up (see rec. 5).

## 4. Four companion mini-game concepts (turn-based, no game loop, no losing)

Every concept uses the **same question engine**. **A correct answer is the only thing that moves the game forward.** After a wrong answer the game shows the correct answer the way Merrick & Fyfe recommend: no red X and no falling tone. The alien says "let's try one like it", and progress simply pauses. Nothing is ever lost.

### A. "Teach Me!": the alien goes to school with you (teachable-agent loop)
- **How it plays.** The alien holds up a question it "can't solve". The child answers, and the alien "learns" it. Each topic (times tables, fractions, place value…) has a **brain-badge track** per alien. A track fills only from correct answers, and a filled track never empties. A full topic gives the alien a visible skill (a calculator badge, a book on its shelf), and 3 topics trigger a **"Scholar" form**.
- **Why it motivates.** The protégé effect: children work harder for an agent, and lower achievers gain most (Chase 2009, strong). Growth mirrors learning, as in My-Pet's open learner model (Chen 2011, strong).
- **Where items help.** Glasses, books and hats unlock a **"hint card"** the alien offers: a worked example or one option removed. Items scaffold. They never answer.
- **Why it is no-lose.** The alien is never "wrong"; it is "still learning". Badges never go down.
- **Cost.** **Lowest of the four**: it reuses the question engine plus per-alien, per-topic counters and some art states. Roughly **2–4 dev days**.

### B. Expedition: explore a planet together (tile map)
- **How it plays.** The child buys supplies (rations, a lantern) with stars and picks an alien to bring. The map is a grid of about 5×5 to 7×7 fogged tiles. **Each correct answer reveals a tile or moves the alien one step.** Tiles hold finds: crystals, fossils, seeds, **postcards** (as in Pikmin Bloom), and new-species clues. Species traits open routes: the swimmer crosses water, the climber crosses cliffs. That gives each of the 6 species a job.
- **Why it motivates.** Exploration and collection, with the maths as the mechanic that moves the game (intrinsic integration: 7× voluntary play, strong). Expeditions bring home things that fill the collection book and decorate the base.
- **Where items help.** A lantern reveals 2 tiles per correct answer. A jetpack hops a crater. A map shows the treasure square. Boots let the alien carry one more find home.
- **Why it is no-lose.** There is no enemy, no running out, and no timer. The expedition ends when the child chooses to "come home", and the alien keeps everything found.
- **Cost.** **Medium**: a seeded tile generator, a find table, CSS reveal transitions, and a save state. Roughly **5–8 dev days**. Turn-based by nature.

### C. Moon Garden: grow new foods and attract visitors (Neko Atsume × Moshi seeds)
- **How it plays.** The child buys seeds with stars and plants them in plots. **Each correct answer is one watering.** A plant blooms after N waterings, with no wilting and no time limit, into **new foods**. That reopens the "find the secret favourite" loop for new aliens and new stages. **Certain three-plant combinations attract a visiting alien**, which can then be adopted or, at minimum, recorded in the book. The combinations are deterministic and hinted.
- **Why it motivates.** It is a stable sink for stars with visible purpose, plus recipe discovery (Moshi Monsters, Neko Atsume). It suits younger children (ages 6–8) in the African edition: very few words to read. Evidence for the genre is weak.
- **Where items help.** A big watering can gives 2 waterings per correct answer. A greenhouse adds plots. A scarecrow hat makes visitors stay longer, cosmetically.
- **Why it is no-lose.** Plants never die and nothing is ever taken away. A wrong answer just means no watering this turn.
- **Cost.** **Low to medium**: a plot grid, a growth counter, a recipe table, and visitor art. Roughly **3–5 dev days**.

### D. Moon Sports Day: obstacle course against your own best
- **How it plays.** A course of 8–10 obstacles in a row, one question per obstacle. A correct answer means the alien **clears the obstacle in style**. A wrong answer means it takes the **slow path around** and still finishes. At the end, a ribbon is given **relative to that alien's own previous best**. There is never any ranking against other children.
- **Why it motivates.** Visible competence and mastery against oneself (weak). It can also host the "talent show" idea.
- **Where items help.** Springy shoes jump higher and grab bonus collectibles above an obstacle. A cape changes the animation. The items affect *how* the alien clears an obstacle, never *whether*.
- **Why it is no-lose.** Every run finishes and every finish earns a ribbon.
- **Risk.** It is the closest of the four to "performance-contingent reward". Keep the ribbons about personal progress, never about speed.
- **Cost.** **Medium**: turn-based, with CSS keyframe animations per obstacle. Roughly **4–7 dev days**.

*(I chose not to recommend a non-violent "battle". Its natural structure is win/lose, so it fights the no-negative-feedback promise. It also brings the Prodigy pay-to-win associations with it.)*

## 5. Recommendation: 8 additions for the Moon, ranked

| # | Addition | Why | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **"Teach Me!" alien school** (concept A) with brain-badge tracks and a **Scholar form** | Gives the aliens what they lost at 18 meals: something to keep becoming. Ties growth to learning. Cheapest to build. | **strong** (protégé effect; My-Pet; feedback studies) |
| 2 | **Expeditions** (concept B) with species traits, finds and postcards | The star sink with purpose the parent asked for. Play *with* the alien, unlimited replays, the maths is the mechanic. Feeds rec. 3. | **strong** for intrinsic integration; **weak** for the expedition pattern itself |
| 3 | **Alien Book (collection book)**: 36 species×colour slots, stages, favourites found, expedition finds, postcards. Partly filled from day one. | Turns "done" into "7 of 36", a long goal she can see. No backend needed. | **ok** (endowed progress is strong in adults; genre practice weak) |
| 4 | **A 4th stage and branching evolutions**, deterministic and hinted. Triggers: learning milestones (from #1), "what it does most" (Tamagotchi Paradise style), favourite food plus an item (Pokémon stone style). | The "evolutions" the parent proposed, built so the child can always see the next step. | **weak** (genre practice); growth-from-effort principle **strong** |
| 5 | **Moon base and habitats**: big-ticket buildings (15–30 sessions each) with a progress bar; each habitat **raises the alien cap**; rooms to decorate | Large prestige sinks. Fixes the 6-alien ceiling without ever asking a child to give an alien up. | **weak** (Animal Crossing, Club Penguin igloos, Webkinz rooms) |
| 6 | **Moon Garden** (concept C): new foods, and recipes that attract new species | A sink that never ends and never decays, and it re-opens the favourite-food discovery. Low reading load for ages 6–8. | **weak** (Neko Atsume / Moshi practice) |
| 7 | **"Always-new" surprise egg** bought with earned stars: no rarity, visible pool, no duplicates (or choose 1 of 3) | A short-term thrill that is legally safe in the UK and EU and ethically clean if not built like a slot machine. | regulation **ok**; harm research **strong** for *paid* boxes only |
| 8 | **More accessories and seasonal sets**, plus a "wear it on an expedition" use | The parent's first idea. It is worth doing, but only after 1–3. On its own it repeats the exhaustion she has already hit. | **weak** |

**Practical notes for the African edition (ages 6–15)** (weak, inference):
- Concepts A–C are turn-based DOM games with no game loop, which suits low-end shared phones.
- Keep instructions icon-led for ages 6–8.
- A single-device save can be wiped when browser storage is cleared. A **"save code" or export** would protect a year of a child's aliens.
- Lower-SES children are the most exposed to manipulative design (Radesky 2022). That makes the "nothing gets worse" promise a real advantage, not a constraint.
